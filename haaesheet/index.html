1Identification & summary

The Howard University African American English (HAAE) dataset is a speech‑and‑transcription corpus of African American English (AAE), collected to close the data gap that causes speech‑language technologies (SLTs) to underperform for Black speakers. Collection began in 2023; the corpus was first released publicly in April 2025. It spans participants across the continental U.S., organized into four regions: Northeast, South, Midwest, West.

Created through a Howard University–Google Research collaboration. Motivation: ASR trained mainly on Mainstream U.S. English (MUSE) yields roughly twice the word error rate for Black speakers as for white speakers, and — apart from CORAAL — little AAE speech data exists to correct this.

TerminologyThe guide deliberately uses MUSE ("Mainstream U.S. English") rather than "Standard American English," rejecting the premise of a single standard variety (Lippi‑Green, 2000; Weissler & Brennan, 2020).

2Motivation

For what purpose was the dataset created?

To improve SLTs for AAE speakers and provide a high‑quality AAE speech resource for NLP, sociolinguistics, and ML research. AAE is a rule‑governed dialect with distinct syntax, verb usage, and phonology, rather than "English with mistakes" (Pullum, 1999), and its under‑representation produces measurable, racialized performance gaps.

Who created and funded it?

HCAI@Howard with Google Research, funded by Howard University, Google Research (award #956968), and the NSF (NR Award N00014‑22‑1‑2714).

Associated publication

Johnson, J., Williams, L., Nias, J., Aryal, S. K., & Washington, G. (2025). Centering Black Voices: Lessons Learned and Reflections from a Large‑Scale AAVE Data Collection. Proc. ACM CHI '25, Yokohama, Japan.

3Composition

What do instances represent?

Each instance is an audio recording of one participant speaking AAE, paired with its human transcription. The published metadata unit is the audio file (schema in §5).

Balance: important for regional modelingRepresentation is heavily uneven between regions (South ≈ 49% of speakers; Midwest ≈ 12%) and within them: one state dominates each region, New York (NE), California (57/73 West), Illinois (28/36 Midwest), DC/GA/LA (South). Several states contribute a single speaker (MS, IN, MN, MO, ID, OR). Region labels are reliable; state‑ and subregion‑level analyses rest on very small samples.

Confidential / identifying data?

Recordings are of identifiable voices plus self‑reported state. Accordingly, biometric voice ID is contractually prohibited (§9) and the data may not be posted to the web. No sex, age, or fine dialect labels are published.

4Collection process

How was the data collected?

By snowball sampling seeded through community activations, gatherings in Black communities on Black culture's impact on technology and the future of AI, held in New Orleans, Washington D.C., New York, Georgia, Illinois, and California. Attendees consented via a digital interest form and shared it onward; those referrals make up most participants.

Who was eligible?

Individuals who self‑identified as African American and had lived in the U.S. ≥ 7 years. State counts came from each participant's self‑reported current state.

Timeframe & consent

Recording spanned Aug 2023 – Jun 2024; release April 1, 2025. Participation was opt‑in via consent/interest form.

5Transcription & labeling

A pilot phase had linguistic experts build a culturally sensitive transcription protocol; a trained professional team transcribed the corpus, with a Howard QC team reviewing ≈10% and weekly meetings to resolve outliers. Baseline transcription follows MUSE orthography while preserving the speaker's words.

AAE grammar written as spoken (preserved)

Invariant be ("He be running"), double negation / negative concord, nonstandard negation ("ain't"), copula omission ("She nice") & variation ("Here go your keys"), absence of ‑s, relative‑pronoun deletion, double modals, past‑participle substitution, nonstandard possessive ("they church"), associative plural ("John and them"), benefactive datives ("get me some food"), subject‑auxiliary order ("Why I can't come").

Phonology normalized to MUSE spelling

Consonant‑cluster reduction (col'→cold), th‑substitution (dis→this), r‑lessness (cah→car), consonant substitution (fitty→fifty), metathesis (aks→ask). Lexical forms kept verbatim: finna, Imma, gonna, talmbout, nem.

Implication for ASR/TTSPronunciation‑level AAE features are normalized in the text but present in the audio. Transcripts describe what was said in MUSE orthography, not how it was pronounced, so acoustic/phonetic dialect signal lives in the waveform, not the transcript. This is the premise behind §6.

6Regional structure & clustering analysis PEBV derived · preliminary

This documents PEBV's own analysis of acoustic regional structure in HAAE‑derived embeddings. It is derived work, not part of the published dataset, included because analyses that affect interpretation should travel with the data.

Working subset

Clustering to date uses a curated subset of speakers with sufficient usable audio (≈77 IDs after filtering), not the full 294‑speaker release. Clips are split into non‑overlapping reference / train / validation partitions (≈404 / 21,887 / 2,395 clips).

Initial finding

K‑means with K=2 on WavLM layer‑11 features, plotted by speaker location, put both clusters at essentially every location, the dominant split is not geographic. In self‑supervised speech features the top axis is usually speaker sex/pitch, channel, or broad phonetic content, which swamp the subtler regional signal. A K=20 GMM (layer 10, UMAP) likewise showed acoustic/phonetic structure and non‑speech islands, not regions.

PEBV region‑clustering pipeline

Validation statusThe pipeline's mechanism is validated on synthetic data mimicking HAAE (a dominant sex axis over a weaker region signal): without nuisance removal, clusters tracked sex (region ARI ≈ 0.26, sex ARI ≈ 0.48); with the full pipeline, region was recovered cleanly (region ARI ≈ 1.0, sex ARI ≈ 0).
Not yet measured on HAAE audioRecovery of region on the real recordings is pending and will be substantially weaker than the synthetic ceiling, because dialect signal is subtle and the §3 imbalance (single‑speaker states; one state dominating each region) limits how well per‑speaker idiosyncrasy averages out. Read results directionally: region agreement should rise and sex agreement fall toward 0 as steps 2–3 are added. A "clean" clustering that merely re‑discovers sex or channel is a failure.

7Uses

Intended uses

Improving SLTs (ASR, TTS, dialogue) for AAE speakers; sociolinguistic and NLP research; and within PEBV, regional speech‑characteristic modeling and voice work representing AAE varieties. A companion PEBV transcription service exposes a fine‑tuned model (built on this data) via authenticated API keys for evaluating AAE transcription against baseline models.

Out of scope / prohibited

See §9: no policing, criminal‑justice, law‑enforcement, surveillance, incarceration, or biometric voice‑ID uses; no technology harming Black, African American, or marginalized groups; no resale, redistribution, web posting, or unpermitted open‑sourcing.

What users should know

The regional imbalance (§3) and the normalization of phonetic features (§5) bound what the data supports. State‑ and subregion‑level claims should be cautious given single‑speaker states; any "AAE performance" evaluation should name the variety and region, since the corpus is not uniform across them.

8Distribution & access

Obtained by formal request and approval at elevateblackvoiceshu.com/data-set. In the current phase, access is granted only to researchers, faculty, and staff at HBCUs; it will broaden over time.

Request requires: signed Fair Use Guidelines (all PIs); institution name; lead PI(s) name/email; the institution's cognizant signee; project title & description; partner affiliations; CITI RCR certification per team member; a data storage & management plan; and a defined data‑use timeframe.

RestrictionsThe data may not be sold, posted to the web (in whole or part), or shared with outside entities. Once approved, neither the data nor any subset may be displayed on any website.

9Fair use, community benefit & harms PEBV core

Access is conditioned on the HAAE Dataset 1.0 Fair Use Guidelines, signed by the lead PI, encoding the PEBV stance that licensing is an instrument of community protection:

  1. No policing, criminal‑justice, or law‑enforcement use, or arresting/restraining/imprisoning Black or African American people.
  2. No biometric recognition/identification of Black or Brown voices.
  3. No resale to any researcher, institution, or organization.
  4. No posting to the web, in whole or in part.
  5. No technology that intentionally harms Black, African American, or marginalized groups.
  6. No sharing of the data with outside entities.
  7. Derived software/technology must directly benefit the Black and/or African American community.
  8. Resulting research must cite the HAAE publication.
  9. Derived works must acknowledge Howard University and the research team.
  10. No open‑sourcing of derived works without Howard's express permission.
  11. Derived software/technology pays Howard a license fee, negotiated with Howard's IP Office & Office of General Counsel.
  12. Any modification of this license requires Howard's approval.
Ethical framingThe guide situates HAAE against a history in which AAE was pathologized (the "deficit hypothesis") and studied narrowly (early focus on urban Black males). HAAE's design: community activations, self‑identification, culturally competent transcription, HBCU‑first access, harm‑preventing licensing, is the corrective: representation on the community's terms, so AAE speakers can "be their authentic selves" with speech technology.

11Provenance & known documentation issues

Sources. §§1–5, 7–10 are drawn from the Howard AAE User Guide v2025.04.01. §6 is PEBV derived analysis and preliminary; its synthetic‑validation figures illustrate pipeline behavior, not measurements on HAAE audio.

Computed values. Totals of 294 speakers and 215,499 files are sums of the four regional figures; they are not stated as totals in the guide.

Discrepancies in the source guide (surfaced for correction)
  1. Every regional table's total row reads "Total of Speakers from the Northeast," including the South/Midwest/West tables, a copy‑paste artifact; the totals (143, 36, 73) are consistent with their state counts.
  2. The narrative says "31 States and DC," but the per‑region tables enumerate 29 states + DC. Kansas, New Mexico, and Ohio appear in the narrative state list (and Ohio in Midwest prose) but not in any per‑region speaker table.
  3. The Midwest and West "About" sentences both begin "The U.S. Southern region…", a template artifact; their state lists/counts are region‑correct.

Gaps not fillable from the guide alone: recording equipment/format & sampling rate; per‑speaker audio counts; age/sex distributions; inter‑annotator agreement; and the license‑fee schedule, these require the HAAE team.
